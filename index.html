Over 99% of USDA samples came in under the legal residue limit, and the Alliance for Food and Farming disputes EWG's ranking method, arguing it counts detections without properly weighting dose. One toxicologist's illustration: you'd need to eat roughly 145 pounds of conventional spinach a day, every serving at the highest residue ever recorded, before reaching a level considered harmful.

EWG agrees with its critics on the part that matters: eat the vegetables. This list is for deciding where a limited organic budget goes — not whether to buy produce at all. Skipping spinach because it ranked first is the worst possible use of this page.

One honest gap: EWG publishes a full ordered ranking of all 47 crops, but only the twelve worst and fifteen cleanest are published as ordered lists. Items marked "middle of the guide" here sit somewhere in between, and this build doesn't claim to know exactly where.

The USDA approves most animal-raising claims on the strength of the producer's own paperwork. As a former Deputy Under Secretary for Food Safety put it, the agency's authority doesn't extend back to the farm — it can't independently confirm an animal was grass fed or free range, so it leans on outside certifiers instead. That's why the seal matters more than the sentence.

Claims that carry a real audit

Independently verified

USDA Organic

Legally defined and third-party inspected. No antibiotics, no added hormones, organic feed. Weak spot: it says little about space per animal or genuine outdoor access.

Independently verified

Animal Welfare Approved (A Greener World)

The strictest of the common seals. Pasture required, no confinement, no antibiotics, no synthetic hormones, audited annually.

Independently verified

American Grassfed Association

Goes well past the USDA grass-fed definition — adds no antibiotics, no synthetic hormones and no confinement, audited through A Greener World.

Independently verified

Certified Humane · Global Animal Partnership

Audited welfare standards. GAP uses a tiered 1–5+ scale, so the number on the tag tells you how far past baseline the farm actually goes.

USDA-audited program

"USDA Process Verified" shield

Not a claim on its own — a shield saying the USDA audited the company's own verification program. Next to a no-antibiotics claim, it makes that claim substantially more trustworthy.

Backed by a signed form and nothing else

Affidavit only

"No antibiotics added" / "Raised without antibiotics"

Legally defined by FSIS but substantiated by affidavit — no independent verification required. Two 2022 studies found the claim failing in practice: one detected antibiotic residues in cattle from 42% of tested "raised without antibiotics" herds; another found drugs in meat certified "no antibiotics ever." Look for a third-party seal or the Process Verified shield alongside it.

Affidavit only

"Grass fed" / "100% grass fed"

USDA definition: never given grain, pasture access during grazing season — documented by written statement, not verified. It leaves a confinement loophole and permits antibiotics and pasture herbicides. Grass fed alone is not a health claim.

Undefined

"Free range" / "Pasture raised"

In its 2024 guidance FSIS explicitly declined to codify what these mean, saying fixed definitions could stifle innovation. Without a certifier's logo beside them, treat them as marketing.

Claims that mean nothing at all

Says nothing about raising

"Natural" / "All Natural"

The most misleading term at the meat case. The USDA definition covers only what happens after slaughter — minimally processed, no artificial ingredients. It tells you nothing about feed, antibiotics, hormones or how the animal lived.

Already required by law

"No hormones added" — on pork or poultry

Federal law already prohibits hormones in pigs and poultry, so the label is describing the legal baseline. Packages must carry a disclaimer saying so, in small print. On beef the claim does mean something.

Loophole phrasing

"No antibiotics used for growth promotion"

Read it closely. Antibiotics may still have been used, just for a different stated reason. Not the same as "raised without antibiotics."

The two-second version at the counter

Ignore the adjectives on the front. Look for a logo with an organization's name on it. If there's no logo, you're reading the producer's own description of themselves, approved by an agency that never visited the farm.

A supermarket meat package is three separate materials, and the variables that actually drive chemical migration are heat, fat and time — not the mere presence of plastic.

What you're holding

The foam tray

Expanded polystyrene — plastic #6, what most people call Styrofoam. Styrene can migrate into food, and how much depends on residual styrene in the material, the food's fat content and pH, storage temperature, and contact time.

EPS · plastic #6

The absorbent pad

Usually silica gel or cellulose sealed inside a perforated plastic film. It pulls liquid away from the meat, which genuinely reduces bacterial growth. Pull it out and bin it when you unwrap — a soaked pad against the meat works against you.

silica or cellulose in film

The clear film

Prepackaged cases usually use polyethylene. Meat wrapped at the store's own counter is more often stretch film, historically PVC with plasticizers. Migration concentrates where film presses against fat — the one contact point worth minimizing.

LDPE or PVC stretch film

The numbers

Time and temperature, not plastic itself

The clearest recent measurements come from dairy packed in polystyrene. Refrigerated yogurt and cream measured 5 to 30 µg/kg of styrene at their best-before date. Single-serve coffee creamer in the same material, held at room temperature for about 190 days, measured 401 µg/kg. Same plastic, an order of magnitude apart, and the only differences are warmth and time.

Raw meat sits cold and gets eaten within days, which puts it at the low end of that range. The European Food Safety Authority is currently re-evaluating styrene for food-contact materials and a restriction is expected — a fair signal that this is a live scientific question, not settled in either direction.

Five things worth actually doing

1 · Never heat anything in the tray

Not the microwave, not a warm car, not the counter while you do something else. Heat is the biggest single multiplier on migration and it's fully under your control.

2 · Repackage before freezing

Freezing takes contact time from days to months. Move it to glass, a freezer bag, or butcher paper. You'll also get less freezer burn, which is the reason a butcher would give you.

3 · Buy from the counter, not the case

Ask for it in paper. Costs nothing, skips the foam tray and stretch film entirely, and you usually get a fresher cut and someone who can tell you where it came from.

4 · Trim fat that touched the film

If a fatty cut sat pressed against stretch wrap, that fat-film interface is where plasticizer concentrates. Trimming it is a thirty-second habit.

5 · Keep it cold and eat it soon

Anticlimactic, but it's what the data supports. Back of the fridge, not the door. The habit that prevents food poisoning also minimizes migration.

Where to send a barcode

Open Food Facts

Free, open-source, no subscription and no commercial incentive to scare you. Scans packaged goods for ingredients, additives and nutrition. The database is community-built, so coverage varies.

world.openfoodfacts.org

Clean Label Project

Free app with results from actual accredited lab testing — heavy metals, 300+ pesticides, glyphosate, bisphenols, phthalates. Best coverage on baby food, formula, protein powder and supplements.

cleanlabelproject.org/app

USDA Pesticide Data Program

The raw source behind the produce tab. Free and public. If you ever want to check the ranking against the underlying numbers yourself, this is where they live.

ams.usda.gov/datasets/pdp

FDA recall alerts

The one genuinely time-sensitive food feed worth subscribing to. Outbreak and recall notices, free by email.

fda.gov recalls

What a scanner genuinely cannot do

Detect adulteration. If a bottle claims pure avocado oil and contains sunflower oil, the label is the lie — catching that takes a mass spectrometer running fatty acid, sterol and tocopherol profiles. A 2026 UC Davis study found 48 of 54 avocado-oil-labeled products adulterated with cheaper oils. No barcode can see through that; only per-lot third-party certification can.

You've probably seen these advertised. They're heavily marketed on Instagram and TikTok, and most are subscription businesses. Here's what they do and don't do.

Reads the label

Yuka · Trash Panda · Ivy · EXPOSR · Olive

These scan a barcode and score the ingredient list. Real value for additives, dyes, allergens, sugar and nutrition — Yuka and Trash Panda do it free. But the method has a hard ceiling: if the label is the lie, no barcode sees through it. Users also report the same product returning different scores on different scans.

good for: additives, allergens, nutrition
Can't help here

Any scanner, for produce or the meat case

Loose produce has no barcode. Meat packages carry a barcode encoding weight and price, not provenance. This entire category is structurally blind to two of the three things you're asking about.

Actual lab data

Clean Label Project

A nonprofit that buys products at retail — not samples brands send in — and tests at Ellipse Analytics, an ISO/IEC 17025 lab accredited by ANAB. Two honest caveats: partly funded by certification fees from brands carrying its mark, and the panel does not include PFAS.

ISO 17025 accredited · ANAB
Reports, not a database

EWG Healthy Living · Mamavation

For PFAS specifically there is no scanner and no lookup. These groups commission testing one category at a time and publish it as an article you read.

What actually works

A free scanner for additives and nutrition, this site for produce and meat, and certification logos as your filter for everything else. A brand paying for per-lot third-party verification has something to lose. A brand writing "natural" on a package does not.
